# poly_division.lean — What It Proves and Why Each Piece Exists

## The Big Picture

The file formalises Chapter 2 of Hassett's *Introduction to Algebraic Geometry*. The central goal is to prove that **every ideal in a multivariate polynomial ring over a field has a unique reduced Gröbner basis**. Think of a Gröbner basis as a particularly well-behaved generating set for an ideal: it makes ideal membership decidable by a generalised long-division algorithm.

The file is organised in ten logical stages. Each stage proves something that the next stage needs.

---

## Stage 1 — The Division Algorithm (`theorem division_algorithm`)

**What it says.** Given any finite list of nonzero polynomials `b₁, …, bₙ` and any polynomial `f`, you can write

```
f = q₁·b₁ + q₂·b₂ + … + qₙ·bₙ + r
```

where the degree of each `qᵢ·bᵢ` does not exceed the degree of `f`, and no monomial of the remainder `r` is divisible by any leading monomial `LM(bᵢ)`.

**Why it is needed.** This is the engine of everything that follows. The membership criterion, Buchberger's criterion, and the existence proofs all reduce questions about ideals to questions about what a division algorithm produces. Without this, there is no constructive handle on ideal membership at all.

**What it wraps.** The actual algorithm lives in Mathlib as `MonomialOrder.div`. This theorem is a convenient re-statement with cleaner hypotheses.

---

## Stage 2 — The Initial Ideal (`def initialIdeal`, three lemmas)

**What it says.** The *initial ideal* `in(I)` of an ideal `I` is the ideal generated by all leading terms of elements of `I`. Three lemmas surround it:

- `leadingTerm_mem_initialIdeal`: the leading term of any `f ∈ I` lies in `in(I)` — this is immediate from the definition.
- `initialIdeal_mono`: if `I ⊆ J` then `in(I) ⊆ in(J)`.
- `span_leadingTerms_le_initialIdeal`: if `G` is any subset of `I`, then the ideal spanned by leading terms of `G` sits inside `in(I)`.

**Why they are needed.** The definition of a Gröbner basis is stated entirely in terms of the initial ideal — `G` is a Gröbner basis for `I` precisely when `in(I)` equals the ideal spanned by leading terms of `G`. The three helper lemmas are used repeatedly to compare these spans.

**The scalar-normalisation lemma** (`span_leadingTerm_eq_span_leadingMonomial`) is the most technical piece here. It says that over a field, the ideal spanned by the leading *terms* (which carry a scalar coefficient) equals the ideal spanned by the leading *monomials* (coefficient 1). This matters because the monomial-ideal membership criterion from Mathlib is stated for monomials, not terms.

---

## Stage 3 — Gröbner Bases (`def IsGroebnerBasis`, four items)

**What it says.** `G` is a Gröbner basis for `I` if every element of `G` lies in `I` and `in(I) = span{LT(g) | g ∈ G}`. Three lemmas extract the two parts, plus one key theorem:

- `leadingDeg_dvd`: for any nonzero `f ∈ I`, there is a nonzero `g ∈ G` whose leading monomial divides the leading monomial of `f`.
- `span_eq`: a Gröbner basis (with no zero elements) generates `I` as an ideal.

**Why they are needed.** `leadingDeg_dvd` is the single most-used fact in the file. It is the reason a Gröbner basis is useful: it lets you always find, for any `f ∈ I`, a basis element whose leading monomial you can cancel. Without it, none of the reduction arguments work.

`span_eq` fills a conceptual gap: the definition only says the *leading term ideal* matches, but it follows that the ideal itself is generated by `G`.

---

## Stage 4 — Reduces-to-Zero (`def ReducesToZero`, two lemmas)

**What it says.** A polynomial `f` *reduces to zero* modulo `b₁, …, bₙ` if it can be written as `f = ∑ qᵢ·bᵢ` where every term `qᵢ·bᵢ` has degree at most `deg(f)` — meaning the division process terminates with no remainder.

- `reducesToZero_zero`: the zero polynomial trivially reduces to zero.
- `mem_span_of_reducesToZero`: if `f` reduces to zero mod the `bᵢ`, then `f` is in the ideal spanned by them.

**Why they are needed.** This is the predicate used in Buchberger's criterion and the membership criterion. It is strictly stronger than just lying in the ideal — it captures the *constructive* witness provided by the division algorithm.

---

## Stage 5 — Ideal Membership Criterion (`theorem mem_ideal_iff_reducesToZero`)

**What it says.** When `G` is a Gröbner basis for `I`, the following are equivalent:
- `f ∈ I`
- `f` reduces to zero when divided by `G`

**Why it is needed.** This is the decision procedure for ideal membership. Without a Gröbner basis you cannot test membership algorithmically — remainders depend on the order in which you reduce. With a Gröbner basis, every sequence of reductions produces the same remainder, and it is zero exactly when `f ∈ I`.

**How it is proved.** The forward direction divides `f` by `G` and shows the remainder is zero using `leadingDeg_dvd`: if the remainder were nonzero and in `I`, some basis element would divide its leading monomial, contradicting the definition of remainder. The backward direction is trivial: if `f = ∑ qᵢgᵢ` and each `gᵢ ∈ I`, then `f ∈ I`.

---

## Stage 6 — Buchberger's Criterion (`theorem buchberger_criterion`)

**What it says.** `G` is a Gröbner basis for `Ideal.span G` if and only if for every pair `g, g' ∈ G`, the *S-polynomial* `S(g, g')` reduces to zero modulo `G`.

**What an S-polynomial is.** `S(g, g')` is a clever linear combination of `g` and `g'` designed so that their leading terms cancel. It is the polynomial obstruction to `G` being a Gröbner basis: if all S-polynomials reduce to zero, there are no obstructions.

**Why it is needed.** Computing the initial ideal directly is infeasible. Buchberger's criterion reduces the Gröbner basis check to a finite number of polynomial divisions — one for each pair in `G`. This is the theoretical basis for the Buchberger algorithm (the algorithm that actually computes Gröbner bases).

**The hard direction.** The `←` direction (all S-polynomials reduce to zero implies Gröbner basis) is the hard part. It requires a well-founded induction on the leading degree: given `f ∈ Ideal.span G`, write `f = ∑ μᵢgᵢ` and show that some `gᵢ` divides `LM(f)`. The key difficulty is when leading terms cancel, which is handled by the private lemmas in Stage 6.

**The private helper chain.** Four private lemmas build up to this:

1. `degree_sum_lt_of_forall_lt`: bounding the degree of a finite sum.
2. `degree_tail_mul_lt_of_top_degree`: the "tail" of a product (strip the leading term) has strictly smaller degree.
3. `degree_sum_lterms_lt_of_cancel`: when top-degree terms cancel in a representation, the sum of their leading term contributions also has smaller degree.
4. `sPolynomial_lterm_mul_reducesToLower`: one S-polynomial between leading-term-scaled basis elements can be reduced to a lower-degree combination using the hypothesis that S-polynomials reduce to zero.
5. `sum_sPolynomial_lterm_reductions`: extends the above to a double sum over all top-degree pairs.
6. `exists_lower_degree_representation_after_top_cancel`: the "boss lemma" — whenever top-degree terms cancel, you can rewrite the representation with a strictly smaller degree bound.
7. `buchberger_key`: packages the well-founded induction using the above, isolated as its own lemma to avoid Lean's elaboration timing out in a large proof state.

---

## Stage 7 — Existence of Gröbner Bases (`theorem groebner_basis_exists`)

**What it says.** For any ideal `I` in `k[x₁,…,xₙ]` (finitely many variables, `k` a field), there exists a finite Gröbner basis.

**Why it is needed.** Without this, Gröbner bases could be a vacuous notion. This is the Hilbert Basis Theorem in action: `k[x₁,…,xₙ]` is Noetherian, so every ideal is finitely generated, and in particular the initial ideal is finitely generated. Lifting generators of the initial ideal back into `I` gives a Gröbner basis.

**How it is proved.** Apply Noetherianity to get finite generators `S` of `in(I)`. Each generator is a leading term of some `f ∈ I` — pick those `f`'s. They form `G`, and by construction their leading terms span `in(I)`.

---

## Stage 8 — Reduced Gröbner Bases (`def IsReducedGroebnerBasis`, helper lemmas)

**What it says.** A Gröbner basis is *reduced* if:
1. Every element is monic (leading coefficient = 1).
2. No monomial of any element `g` is divisible by the leading monomial of any other element `g'`.

The helper lemmas for *uniqueness* are:
- `rgb_ne_zero`: every element of a reduced GB is nonzero (monic implies nonzero).
- `rgb_degree_mem_support`: the leading monomial is in the support of each element.
- `rgb_no_lm_div`: no two distinct elements have one's leading monomial dividing the other's.
- `rgb_diff_lt`: if two monic polynomials share the same leading monomial but are unequal, their difference has strictly smaller degree.
- `rgb_lm_match`: for any element `g` of one reduced GB, some element of another reduced GB for the same ideal has the same leading monomial.
- `rgb_element_eq`: two elements from different reduced GBs of the same ideal, sharing the same leading monomial, must be equal.
- `rgb_subset`: every reduced GB is a subset of every other reduced GB for the same ideal.

**Why these are needed.** They build a chain of reasoning culminating in `rgb_subset`. Applying `rgb_subset` in both directions gives the uniqueness theorem.

---

## Stage 9 — Existence of Reduced Gröbner Bases (three private lemmas + `rgb_exists`)

**`gb_erase_zero`** — Why it is needed. A Gröbner basis may contain the zero polynomial, which contributes nothing (its leading term is zero). This lemma shows that erasing zero preserves the Gröbner basis property. It is the first cleanup step in the minimisation process.

**`gb_erase_dominated`** — Why it is needed. In a Gröbner basis, if `LM(g₁) | LM(g₀)` (i.e., `g₁`'s leading monomial divides `g₀`'s), then `LT(g₀)` is already in the ideal spanned by `LT(g₁)`, so `g₀` is redundant. This lemma shows you can erase `g₀` without losing the Gröbner basis property. It is the second cleanup step.

**`degree_smul_of_ne`** — Why it is needed. Scaling a nonzero polynomial by a nonzero scalar does not change its leading monomial. This is needed to verify that the leading monomial is preserved when normalising elements to be monic.

**`gb_minimal_exists`** — Why it is needed. Starting from any Gröbner basis, this lemma produces a *minimal* one: no zeros, no dominated elements. It proceeds by well-founded induction on the cardinality of `G`: at each step, either erase a zero (using `gb_erase_zero`) or erase a dominated element (using `gb_erase_dominated`), each of which strictly decreases the size.

**`gb_monic_minimal_exists`** — Why it is needed. Given a minimal Gröbner basis, produce a monic minimal one by dividing each element by its leading coefficient. This requires showing that the image under `g ↦ LC(g)⁻¹ · g` still generates the same initial ideal (the leading monomials are unchanged by `degree_smul_of_ne`), each element is now monic, and minimality is preserved.

**`gb_reduce_to_rgb`** — Why it is needed. Given a monic minimal Gröbner basis, produce a *reduced* one by dividing each element `g` by the rest of the basis. This is the most complex step: for each `g ∈ G`, divide `g` by `G \ {g}` to get a remainder `r`. The minimality of `G` ensures `r` has the same leading monomial as `g` (no other basis element can cancel it), so `r` is still monic with the same degree. The new basis `G'` consists of these remainders; it inherits the Gröbner and monic properties, and every monomial of each `r` not divisible by any other basis element's leading monomial — that is exactly the reduced condition.

**`rgb_exists`** — Why it is needed. This is the capstone: every nonzero ideal has a reduced Gröbner basis. It chains the three existence lemmas: start with `groebner_basis_exists`, pass through `gb_minimal_exists`, `gb_monic_minimal_exists`, and `gb_reduce_to_rgb`.

---

## Stage 10 — Uniqueness (`theorem rgb_unique`)

**What it says.** The reduced Gröbner basis of an ideal `I` is unique.

**How it follows.** `rgb_subset` shows any reduced GB is a subset of any other. Applied in both directions, the two sets must be equal.

---

## Summary of the Logical Flow

```
Division Algorithm
       ↓
  Initial Ideal  ←  Scalar normalisation lemma
       ↓
  Gröbner Basis definition + leadingDeg_dvd
       ↓
  Membership Criterion   ←   Reduces-to-Zero
       ↓
  Buchberger's Criterion  (6 helper lemmas)
       ↓
  Existence of Gröbner Bases  (Hilbert Basis Theorem / Noetherianity)
       ↓
  Reduced GB definition + uniqueness helpers
       ↓
  Existence of Reduced GB  (erase zeros → erase dominated → make monic → reduce)
       ↓
  Uniqueness of Reduced GB
```

Every arrow represents a proof that genuinely uses the thing above it — no step is cosmetic.
